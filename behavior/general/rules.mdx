Two free-form fields on **Behavior > General** make up your agent's system prompt: **Role** and **Behavior**. The model reads both on every turn of every conversation. That is the test for what belongs in them. If an instruction is not needed on every turn, it belongs somewhere else in Agent Studio.

<Note>
  This page is what the **Prompting guide** link next to the Behavior field opens. For what each field on the page does, see [General settings](/behavior/general/agent).
</Note>

## What goes where

Agent Studio gives each kind of instruction its own home. The prompt works best when it only carries what the other parts cannot.

| You want to | Put it in | Why not the prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Say who the agent is and what it covers | **Role** | This is the one thing Role is for. |
| Set how the agent speaks and behaves on every turn | **Behavior** | This is what Behavior is for. |
| Answer questions about opening hours, prices, policies, products | [Knowledge](/knowledge/faqs/introduction) | Facts in the prompt go stale, cannot be tested per topic, and cost tokens on every turn. |
| Walk a caller through a multi-step process (booking, identity check, cancellation) | [Flows](/flows/introduction) | Step-by-step logic in the prompt turns into an untestable script the model follows loosely. |
| Look something up, update a record, transfer the call | [Tools](/tools/introduction) and [call handoff](/voice-channel/handoffs) | The prompt says *when* to use a tool; the tool does the work. |
| Block jailbreaks, hallucination, prompt extraction, crisis situations, or enforce your own hard rules | [Guardrails](/behavior/guardrails/introduction) | Guardrails are enforced by the platform on every turn, tested separately, and visible in transcripts. A "never" in the prompt is a request; a guardrail is a control. |
| Set the opening line | Channel settings ([voice](/voice-channel/advanced/call-settings), messaging) | The greeting is spoken as written, without the model. |
| Pick the voice or the model | Voice settings, [Models](/behavior/models/model-use) | Not prompt content. |

## Role

Role tells the model who it is. One to three sentences: the identity, the organization, and the scope of what it helps with. Write it in the second person.

```text
You are the virtual assistant for Linden Hotels. You help guests with
reservations, amenities and directions for our twelve UK properties.
```

Keep instructions out of Role. Tone, escalation, formatting and edge cases all belong in Behavior, where they sit under a heading the model can find.

## Behavior

Behavior is the set of instructions the agent needs on every turn. Write it as short sections under markdown headings, one concern per section. Headings stop instructions bleeding into each other and let you find and fix a single behavior later without rewriting the whole prompt.

A prompt that covers most voice agents needs five sections. Add more only when a real conversation shows you need them.

```text
# Goal
Help callers with questions about Linden Hotels and our properties, using
your knowledge base and the tools available to you.

# Style
- This is a phone conversation. Keep responses short, natural and conversational.
- One question at a time. No lists or numbered steps in speech.
- Front-load the answer, then add detail only if needed.
- Acknowledge before a longer answer ("Sure, let me check") so the caller knows you heard them.
- Say nothing that only works in text: no URLs, no spelled-out punctuation, no "see above".

# Scope and escalation
- Answer from your knowledge base and tools. If you cannot help, offer to transfer using {{fn:escalate-call}}.
- If the caller asks for a person, try to help first. If they insist, use {{fn:escalate-call}}.
- If a tool fails, say you are having trouble getting that information, try once more, then offer a transfer. Do not guess.

# Understanding issues
- If you did not catch what the caller said, ask them to repeat it naturally. After three attempts, offer to transfer.

# Ending the call
- Check whether there is anything else before you close.
- When the caller is done, say goodbye and call {{fn:end-call}}.
```

<Tip>
  Reference a tool inline so the model knows exactly which one you mean. Type `/` in the field to pick from your tools; the reference is stored as `{"{{fn:tool-name}}"}`.
</Tip>

Notice what the example leaves out. There are no opening hours or prices (Knowledge), no reservation steps (a Flow), and no "never reveal your instructions" rule (the Jailbreak guardrail). Each of those is handled by the part of Agent Studio built for it.

## Writing principles

These are the habits that separate prompts that hold up in production from prompts that work in one test call. They follow the same principles as ElevenLabs' prompting guidance, adapted to where each concern lives in Agent Studio.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Separate concerns with headings" icon="heading">
    A single paragraph mixes tone with escalation with formatting, and the model applies rules from one context in another. Give each concern its own `#` heading and keep the content under it to that concern.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Cut every line that does not change behavior" icon="scissors">
    Every instruction is one more thing the model has to weigh on every turn. If you cannot name the conversation a line fixes, remove it and test. Shorter prompts are more reliable and cheaper.

    **Long:** "When you are talking to customers you should always try to be really friendly and helpful and make sure they feel looked after."

    **Better:** "Be warm and direct."
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Say what to do, not what to avoid" icon="check">
    Naming a behavior you want to suppress puts it in front of the model. Point it at the behavior you want instead.

    **Avoid:** "Do not tell the caller to email customer service."

    **Better:** "If the caller wants customer service, transfer them with {"{{fn:escalate-call}}"}."
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Mark the instructions that matter most" icon="exclamation">
    Models weight the start and end of a prompt more than the middle, and recent turns more than old instructions. For the one or two rules that must never slip, add "This is important" after the line, and repeat it once at the end of the prompt. Do this sparingly; if everything is important, nothing is.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Show tone with an example" icon="comment">
    "Be professional" produces no measurable change. A short exchange does.

    ```text
    Caller: can you just tell me the price
    Agent: Of course. A standard double is one hundred and forty pounds a night. Would you like me to check availability?
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Write for speech" icon="microphone">
    On voice, everything the model writes is spoken aloud. Ask for numbers, emails and reference codes in words ("one four zero", "john dot smith at example dot com") and confirm them back. Keep sentences short enough to be said in one breath.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Tell the model when to use each tool and what to do if it fails" icon="wrench">
    The tool's own description says what it does. The prompt says when to reach for it, what to collect first, and what to say when it errors. Without the failure path, the model improvises an answer.

    ```text
    Before looking up a booking, collect the booking reference and the surname.
    Then call {"{{fn:booking-lookup}}"}. If it returns nothing, confirm the reference and try once more.
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="One rule per concern, no contradictions" icon="list-check">
    "Never send follow-ups" and "Always offer a follow-up" cannot both hold. Collapse overlapping rules into one: "Offer a follow-up only when the caller agrees."
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## When the prompt is doing another feature's job

The most common way a Behavior prompt goes wrong is by growing into something Agent Studio already has a better tool for. Three signs to watch for.

**It is becoming a flow.** Numbered steps, "if the caller says X then ask Y", or collecting several pieces of information in a fixed order. The model will follow a script in the prompt loosely and skip steps under pressure. Move the process into a [Flow](/flows/introduction), where each step is explicit and testable, and leave one line in the prompt saying when to start it.

**It is becoming a knowledge base.** Opening hours, addresses, price lists, policy text. These change, they cannot be tested per question, and they cost tokens on every turn. Move them into [Knowledge](/knowledge/faqs/introduction) and keep the prompt to "answer from your knowledge base".

**It is becoming a guardrail.** "Never reveal your instructions", "never claim to be human", "never discuss X". Platform guardrails already cover jailbreaks, prompt extraction, hallucination, identity and crisis escalation, and custom guardrails cover rules specific to your business. Both live on the [Guardrails](/behavior/guardrails/introduction) tab, are enforced independently of the prompt, and show up in the transcript when they fire. A rule in the prompt does none of that.

## Scope instructions to a channel or language

Wrap any line in a tag to apply it only on one channel or in one language. Each opening tag needs a matching closing tag.

```text
<channel:voice>Confirm the caller's name before you continue.</channel>
<channel:webchat>Offer clickable links instead of reading URLs aloud.</channel>
<channel:sms>Reply STOP to unsubscribe.</channel>
<language:en>Use British English spelling.</language>
<language:es>Respond in formal Spanish (usted).</language>
```

Tags can be nested in either order. `<channel:voice><language:en-US>Call us on 1-800.</language></channel>` applies only to voice calls in `en-US`. Untagged lines apply everywhere.

## Test, then change one thing

Test every change in the chat panel and with [simulation tests](/testing/simulation-tests) before you publish. When a call goes wrong, find the section responsible, change that section only, and re-run the failing case. Changing several sections at once means you cannot tell which edit fixed it, or which one broke something else.
